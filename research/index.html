Research interests

Robotics and mechatronics, astrophysics, and applied physics.

Photos and clips from my robotics and physics work.

Robotics and mechatronics

I started P.H.A.S.E. with software. I spent my first month of work building a system that could identify a card in a perfect environment, then realised I needed to move on to the physical work. I split the machine into three parts and worked on one at a time until I was satisfied with its functioning.

The moment I remember best is the first time something worked in reality after hours and hours of work: a stepper motor moving for the first time, or the code making a correct guess. That payoff is the feeling I still chase.

The software nearly beat me. I never expected the codebase to pass 2,000 lines, and it grew to about 13,000. Two weeks before presenting, the machine still consistently failed to identify the card, because it couldn’t differentiate the card borders from the background. I trained a machine learning model (YOLO) to fix just that. If I were to do it again, I’d start with easier projects. What I enjoy most is overcoming a challenge I once had no idea how to start. By doing smaller projects, I could have built up to complex systems more gradually.

I start any given project for two reasons: it’s something I genuinely think is cool, and it requires skills I have yet to acquire. The custom lamp involved addressable LED control. The jukebox was my introduction to mechatronic teamwork, plus audio circuits and chip detection. That's also why I'm learning CAD and PCB design now. Next, I want to work on something that pushes the bounds of my own knowledge, and of what's possible more broadly.

Astrophysics

Space is the subject I've wanted to work in since I was young. Science always felt like something I could do one day, and space was the part I could picture myself in. If I could spend my life researching it, that would have been enough for me as a kid.

I've been lucky to experience it up close. In my Astronomy and Astrophysics class at Champlain, some of our late-night labs were replaced by observation sessions on the roof of the building, using our professor's own small telescope. His passion was infectious. Other times we went to the Bishop's University observatory to see Cepheid variable stars, the constellations, and Sirius, the “disco star”, whose light flickers through colours as it crosses our turbulent atmosphere. We also took a trip to the Mont-Mégantic observatory, the kind of experience that sticks with you for life. Across those nights I saw several different galaxies for myself, each one an entire system of billions of stars, which is something I never take for granted.

Anyone can watch a documentary about what's already established, or about the theories. I want to be part of the work behind it: to find out what's out there, and why what we see happens. I haven't done astrophysics research yet, and I'd love to change that. I find this kind of pursuit deeply fulfilling, and one day I'd like to share that passion with the people around me.

Applied physics

I already liked physics, but Waves and Modern Physics is what turned it into a real passion. My professor was the same one who taught Astronomy and Astrophysics, and his enthusiasm was just as infectious. The first lab I remember was on wave harmonics, getting strings to oscillate at different frequencies. I did it with my best friend, and I was blown away to see the predicted results, nodes and all, actually happen in the experiment, when they had only ever seemed believable on paper. Every new topic in that class felt just as interesting, because each one was part of the basis of how everything works.

I came back to that passion in Experimental Physics, one of the most enjoyable classes I've taken. Weekly evaluations and labs made up most of the grade, but I enjoyed the work too much for it to feel stressful. Many of the labs were on light physics, and those stood out, especially spectroscopy and holography. In a holography lab, I got to engrave a developing plate with light interference, and I thought it was just the coolest thing I’d ever seen. The setup required absolutely no perturbations, zero outside light, and lengthy exposure times. In spite of this, my best friend and I went back to the lab after hours several times to produce holograms from 3D printed figures of friends and family as gifts.

The jukebox was where I got to apply all of it. It was my final circuit project in Experimental Physics, and the physics behind it came straight from Electricity and Magnetism: the NFC chip in each disc has no power of its own and is energised by the reader through electromagnetic induction, and the circuit around it relied on the design principles from that course. It was exceptionally rewarding to translate what I had studied into a working project.
