Project · 2025 – Winter 2026

P.H.A.S.E.

Perceptual Hashing for an Automated Sorting Engine. A machine that sorts Magic: The Gathering cards. Load up to 450 cards; it identifies each one, looks up its price, and sorts it into a high-value, low-value or unidentified box.

Why I built it

I own more than 13,000 Magic cards, collected over about a decade, and there are over 110,000 unique printings of Magic cards in existence. Sorting a collection by hand takes weeks. Machines that do it already exist, but they cost thousands of dollars and charge monthly software fees, which puts them out of reach for hobbyists.

My hypothesis was that I could get comparable results for far less money. It was also my first electronics project. I had never modelled anything in 3D and had barely programmed. It took 550+ hours over a calendar year, and it's the reason I chose engineering.

Built solo, and finished as my Integrative Project in Science at Champlain College Lennoxville (Winter 2026).

How it works

The machine

Cards sit in a holding area, up to 450 stacked vertically. A NEMA 17 stepper raises the platform and a TT motor releases one card at a time down a slide into an enclosed imaging area. LED strips at 45° keep the lighting even and cut glare. A servo stops the card under a USB camera. Once it's identified, a second stepper on a threaded rod slides a container over the right box and a servo lets the card go.

The whole machine runs from one Arduino Uno driving two NEMA 17 steppers, two MG90S servos and one TT motor, and the heavy computing happens on a laptop over USB. It was my first electronics project, so the wiring is not pretty. I soldered the circuits myself, and each of the 60 unique 3D printed parts was modelled in Blender, printed on a Prusa Mini+ and plastic-welded together.

Finding the card

Before identifying anything, the software has to find the card. I trained YOLOv8 on about 1,400 card samples. It detects whether a card is present, and estimates where it is, with roughly 93% location accuracy. That isn't tight enough for hashing, so a contrast-masking step samples the background above and below the card, removes those colours, and uses what's left to find the card's exact edges and its rotation. The image is then rotated and cropped to the card.

Identifying it

Perceptual hashing turns an image into a small black-and-white grid, 8 by 8, that reflects what it looks like. I made one for every Magic printing. Identification runs in two stages:

  • Candidate search. A hash of the whole card is compared against the database using the Annoy library, narrowing 100,000+ printings to the 1,000 most likely.
  • Verification. The top half of the card is cut into a 3×3 grid and each square is hashed and compared against those 1,000 candidates. If the match isn’t confident enough, the card goes to the unidentified box.

Once a card is identified, the Scryfall API gives its current price and the machine routes it by value. The code is around 13,000 lines across Python, C, C++, HTML and JavaScript. The repository is on GitHub.

Did it work?

P.H.A.S.E. cost CAD $410.39 to build and sorts 300 to 375 cards per hour. In my own testing on my collection it identified cards with 100% accuracy. I haven't published that test set, so I'd treat the figure as a result from one collection and not a benchmark.

Limitations

The system tells apart printings from different sets, but not different languages of the same card. It was built for one machine (a Windows laptop and a specific Arduino setup) and I haven't tested it elsewhere. The databases it needs are more than 160 MB, so it isn't something you can clone and run.

Awards

I presented P.H.A.S.E. at the Expo-sciences Estrie regional finals (March 2026) and the Super Expo-sciences Hydro-Québec provincial finals (April 2026). It won:

  • Gold Medal, Expo-sciences Estrie regional finals (March 2026). Best project among all collegiate presentations. This put P.H.A.S.E. on the Estrie delegation to the provincial finals.
  • Ubisoft Prize, Super Expo-sciences Hydro-Québec provincial finals (April 2026). Given to one engineering, robotics and software project at the senior or collegiate level.
  • CGI Prize for Engineering, Software and Robotics, regional finals (March 2026). Innovation through software integrated in a project.
  • Prix du Réseau ACTION TI, regional finals (March 2026). Best design and application of software at the collegiate level.

The Prix du Réseau ACTION TI also came with a nomination for the OCTAS Youth Prize in Information Technology.
